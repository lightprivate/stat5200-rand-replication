2. Research Question and Replication Goal

The question is whether higher out-of-pocket costs reduce medical care use and spending. I use the public RAND data to compare cost-sharing plans with free care. I examine whether people have any medical spending and how much they spend, separately for total, inpatient, and outpatient care. I reproduce the authors’ estimates in R, then check how they change after adjusting for unreported claims and participants’ characteristics before the experiment.

Sources: Aron-Dine, Einav, and Finkelstein (2013), https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.27.1.197 ; authors’ public data and tables.do, https://doi.org/10.3886/E113919V1 .

3. Background: The RAND Health Insurance Experiment

The RAND Health Insurance Experiment was conducted in the 1970s and 1980s. Families were randomly assigned to insurance plans with different levels of cost sharing. Some received free care, while others had to pay part of their medical costs, subject to out-of-pocket limits.

The random assignment is important because it helps make the groups comparable before treatment. This gives us a stronger basis for attributing differences in medical care use and spending to the insurance plans, rather than to pre-existing differences between families.

In their 2013 paper, the authors revisited this experiment, examining its findings, potential limitations, and implications for policy. Their comparisons of medical care use and spending across insurance plans are the starting point for my replication.

Source: Aron-Dine, Einav, and Finkelstein (2013). https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.27.1.197

5. Empirical Approach

I estimate six regressions: whether there is any spending and the amount spent, separately for total, inpatient, and outpatient care.

In this equation, Y is a person’s outcome in a given year. Each beta measures the difference between a cost-sharing plan and free care. The plan indicator equals one for that plan and zero otherwise. The control terms account for site-by-enrollment-month groups and enrollment year.

I estimate each model using ordinary least squares, or OLS. For the yes-or-no outcomes, this is a linear probability model, so the coefficients represent differences in probability.

Standard errors are clustered by family because observations from the same family, including repeated observations over time, may be correlated.

Sources: Aron-Dine, Einav, and Finkelstein (2013), https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.27.1.197 ; authors’ public data and tables.do, https://doi.org/10.3886/E113919V1 .

This table presents the baseline results reproduced in R. The free-care row reports adjusted levels, while the other rows report differences relative to free care. The numbers in parentheses are family-clustered standard errors, which measure uncertainty in the estimates.

To illustrate the results, I’ll focus on the 95 percent coinsurance plan, highlighted in red. Compared with free care, the probability of having any medical spending is 17 percentage points lower. Annual total spending is 845 dollars lower per person. Relative to the adjusted free-care level of 2,170 dollars, this is a reduction of about 39 percent.

Outpatient spending is also lower by 629 dollars. The evidence for inpatient spending is less clear overall: the joint-test p-value is about 0.154, so we cannot reject the hypothesis that all five plan effects on inpatient spending are zero at the five percent level. This does not prove that there is no effect.

All 36 coefficient estimates match the paper at its reported precision. Two small display differences involve an inpatient standard error and the inpatient joint p-value; two-stage rounding may explain them. The table shown here uses the reconstructed paper-style display.

Overall, the answer is yes: compared with free care, requiring patients to share the cost reduces the likelihood of using medical care and lowers total medical spending. The evidence is clearer for outpatient spending, while the effects on inpatient spending are less certain.

8. Sensitivity Analysis: Why and How

The earlier results show lower recorded spending under cost sharing. I now check how these estimates change when I adjust for missing claims and participants’ characteristics before the experiment.

One concern is that participants who pay a larger share of their bills may have less incentive to submit claims. Some care they received may therefore be missing from the records, making the spending reduction appear larger than it really is.

I continue to use the same RAND data on participants’ annual medical spending. I focus on the difference between the 95 percent coinsurance plan and free care, examining both whether any spending occurs and how much is spent.

I compare three versions of the analysis. First, I keep the original model as a starting point. Second, I adjust reported spending and use of care using the authors’ plan-specific correction factors. Third, I keep those corrections and add characteristics measured before the experiment, including income, demographics, health, and prior use of care. This checks whether observable differences among participants affect the estimates.

All three versions retain the site and enrollment-timing controls and family-clustered standard errors. I compare how much the estimated differences change, while recognizing that these adjustments cannot address every possible source of bias.

10. Conclusion

To conclude, I reproduced the authors’ estimates of how insurance plans affect medical care use and spending, then checked their sensitivity to reporting corrections and participants’ baseline characteristics. Cost sharing is associated with lower spending in these experimental comparisons, with clearer evidence for outpatient spending. The sensitivity results preserve the main direction, but they also show that effect sizes depend on the adjustment assumptions.

There is still more to do. I start from cleaned data, and I have not reproduced the selection bounds. Those would be a further step in assessing robustness.

For my own project, I would then ask whether responses differ by baseline family income. The same medical bill may matter differently for households with different resources. I would check income measurement and group sizes, add plan-by-income interactions, and test the differences directly while retaining family-clustered inference. This is a proposed next step, not a finding from the current replication.
